### Observations from exceptional months

Looking across the tables, many of the largest warm anomalies occur in the later part of the record, particularly after 2000. At the same time, earlier years still appear frequently. For example, **February 1779** remains the warmest February on record for Central England, and several of the most extreme June values come from the **19th century** rather than the modern period.

Cold anomalies show a different pattern. The coldest months are spread fairly evenly across much of the record, especially before the early 20th century. This suggests that very cold months have occurred at irregular intervals throughout the series, rather than being concentrated in one specific period.

In more recent decades, fewer months appear among the most extreme cold records. **1947** remains the most recent year to hold records for the coldest individual months. **December 2010** is the only post-2000 month to place within the top three cold anomalies for any calendar month, whether the instrumental or full record is used. Its presence shows that exceptional cold months are still possible, even if they occur less often.

These rankings focus on individual extremes, but a single exceptional month does not describe conditions before or after it. Looking only at warm records can also hide the full range of variation present in the data, especially when cold anomalies are considered alongside them.

Differences between seasons are also visible. The range of outcomes appears wider in winter than in summer, with larger departures from typical conditions. The next section explores this seasonal behaviour in more detail by focusing specifically on winter temperatures.
